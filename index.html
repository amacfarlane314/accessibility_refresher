Section 1

Why It Still Matters

Digital accessibility isn't a trend — it's a legal obligation and a civil right. These laws apply directly to the content you create as a district employee.

Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA)

Prohibits discrimination based on disability in public services. Courts have consistently ruled that websites and digital content are covered.

Section 504 — Rehabilitation Act

Requires programs receiving federal funding to provide equal access to individuals with disabilities. Applies to your district's digital communications.

Section 508 — Rehabilitation Act

Federal standard for accessible electronic and information technology. Sets the technical baseline referenced in WCAG 2.1 Level AA.

IDEA

Individuals with Disabilities Education Act ensures students with disabilities receive a free, appropriate public education — including accessible materials.

Colorado HB 21-1110

Colorado's own web accessibility law requiring state and local government digital content to meet WCAG 2.1 Level AA — and it covers your district.

WCAG 2.2 — The Standard

Web Content Accessibility Guidelines set by the W3C. Level AA is the current legal and ethical benchmark for all digital content your district publishes.

The bottom line: Whether you're at a school site or the district office — creating an email, a Google Form, a PDF, or a slide deck — accessibility requirements apply to your work. Non-compliance creates real barriers for real people and exposes the district to legal risk.

Section 2

Empathy in Action

Laws and guidelines are important — but the reason we care about accessibility is people. Read these two scenarios and take a moment to sit with each one.

👩‍💼
Maria, Administrative Assistant
Central Office — Finance Department

Maria has been with the district for eleven years. Last fall, she experienced significant vision loss in one eye following a medical emergency. She now relies on a screen magnifier and, increasingly, a screen reader to get through her day.

This week, a budget summary PDF was shared via email for an all-staff meeting. The file had been exported from a spreadsheet — no reading order defined, no column headers tagged, all the data presented as one flat image. Maria's screen reader read the contents as a single unbroken string of numbers with no context.

She spent 45 minutes trying to reconstruct the table before the meeting started. She attended unprepared — not because she wasn't capable, but because the document wasn't built for how she now has to read.

💬 "I used to be the one who caught every number. Now I spend my energy just trying to get to the information."

🎒
DeShawn, 9th Grader
High School — English & Biology

DeShawn has dyslexia. He uses text-to-speech software every day — on his Chromebook for assignments, on his phone for anything longer than a paragraph. It's not a workaround; it's just how he reads best.

His biology teacher posted a study guide as a scanned image of a printed worksheet — a photo of paper, uploaded to the class page as a JPEG. When DeShawn tried to use his text-to-speech tool, it found nothing to read. To his software, the guide was just a picture.

He asked a friend to read it aloud on a video call the night before the test. His friend didn't have time for the whole thing. DeShawn showed up to the exam having reviewed less than half the material.

💬 "I study really hard. But sometimes it feels like the work is figuring out how to get to the assignment, not doing the assignment."

Pause and reflect: Maria and DeShawn aren't edge cases — they represent the range of people in your district who rely on accessible content every single day. The documents you create, the emails you send, the slides you share: each one is an opportunity to include everyone, or to quietly leave someone behind.

Accessibility isn't about compliance checklists. It's about doing right by your colleagues and your students.

Section 3

POUR at a Glance

Every WCAG guideline maps back to one of four principles. If you understand POUR, you understand the why behind every rule you apply.

P

Perceivable P

Information must be presentable in ways users can perceive — it can't be invisible to all of their senses.

  • Alt text on images
  • Captions on videos
  • Sufficient color contrast
  • No information conveyed by color alone
O

Operable O

Interface components and navigation must be operable — users must be able to use content, even without a mouse.

  • Full keyboard navigation
  • Visible focus indicators
  • No seizure-triggering content
  • Clear, descriptive page titles
U

Understandable U

Information and interfaces must be understandable — users can comprehend both content and how to navigate it.

  • Clear, plain language
  • Descriptive link text
  • Consistent navigation
  • Meaningful error messages
R

Robust R

Content must be reliable enough to be interpreted by a wide variety of assistive technologies — now and in the future.

  • Valid, semantic structure
  • Proper heading hierarchy
  • Form fields with labels
  • Programmatic status messages

Quick gut-check: Before sharing any digital content, ask: Can everyone perceive it? Can they operate it without a mouse? Will they understand it clearly? Is it robust enough for assistive tech? Four yeses and you're in good shape.

Section 4

The Big 5 Practices

These five practices address the most common accessibility failures in everyday digital content. Master these, and you'll eliminate the majority of barriers in what you create.

Use Headings as Structure, Not Style

Headings are the skeleton of your document. Screen readers use them as navigation landmarks — users can jump directly from heading to heading. A heading chosen because it "looks bold" breaks that structure entirely.

The rule: Use heading levels in logical order (H1 → H2 → H3). Never skip levels. Never use a heading just because you like how it looks.

✗ Inaccessible

Meeting Notes — April

Bold text used as a "heading"

Bold or large text looks like a heading but has no semantic meaning. A screen reader reads it as a paragraph — no navigation, no structure.

✓ Accessible

Meeting Notes — April
Agenda Items
Budget Review
Body text here…

Real heading styles create navigable structure for screen readers and other assistive tools.

Quick apply: In Word, use the Styles pane (Heading 1, Heading 2…). In Google Docs, use the Paragraph Styles dropdown. Don't use Bold + font size — use actual heading styles.

Write Alt Text That Actually Describes

Alternative text is read aloud by screen readers in place of an image. Its purpose is to convey the meaning and function of the image — not just describe it literally.

The rule: Describe what the image communicates. Decorative images get an empty alt attribute (alt="") so screen readers skip them.

✗ Inaccessible

alt="image"alt="photo.jpg"alt="graph"

These tell a screen reader user nothing about what they're missing.

✓ Accessible

alt="Bar graph showing 2024 student enrollment by school, with Jefferson Elementary highest at 612 students" alt="Staff member demonstrates ergonomic desk setup with monitor at eye level"

Describe the meaning. Include key data from charts. Be specific enough that a non-sighted user gets the same information.

Quick apply: Right-click any image → "Edit Alt Text." Ask: if someone can't see this, what do they need to know? For purely decorative images, leave alt text blank — don't delete the attribute.

Contrast Isn't Just Aesthetics — It's Access

Low contrast makes content difficult or impossible to read for people with low vision, color blindness, or even in bright sunlight.

The rule (WCAG AA): Normal text needs at least 4.5:1. Large text (18pt+ or bold 14pt+) needs at least 3:1.

This text fails.

~1.6:1 ✗

This text passes.

~14.7:1 ✓

This text passes.

~12.1:1 ✓

Large text only.

~3.1:1 (large ✓)

Quick apply: Use the free WebAIM Contrast Checker before finalizing any design. Navy-on-white passes comfortably — watch out for grays, light blues, and yellow on white.

Link Text Should Make Sense Out of Context

Screen reader users often navigate by jumping between links — they hear only the link text, not the surrounding sentence. Vague link text is a wall.

The rule: Link text must describe the destination clearly enough to stand alone. Avoid "click here," "read more," and raw URLs.

✗ Inaccessible

For the staff handbook, click here.Read more.https://district.org/handbook2024-final-v3.pdf

Meaningless without surrounding context — which a screen reader user navigating by links won't have.

✓ Accessible

Download the 2024–25 Staff Handbook (PDF)Read the full district accessibility policyRegister for the spring professional development workshop

Clear, specific, self-contained. A user jumping between links knows exactly what each one does.

Quick apply: Before finalizing any document or email, skim just the links. Could you tell where each one goes with no other context? If not, rewrite the anchor text itself.

If It Has Audio, It Needs Captions

Captions aren't just for Deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers. They help English language learners, people in noisy or quiet environments, and anyone who learns better with text and audio together.

The rule: All prerecorded video with audio must have accurate, synchronized captions. Auto-generated captions are a starting point — never a finished product.

✗ Inaccessible

Unreviewed auto-captions:

"their going too the library too check out books four students"

Auto-captions can be wildly inaccurate, especially with proper nouns, technical terms, or accents.

✓ Accessible

Reviewed, corrected captions:

"They're going to the library to check out books for students."

Reviewed for accuracy, correct punctuation, and speaker identification when needed.

Quick apply: YouTube, Google Meet, and Microsoft Stream all generate auto-captions — always review and correct before sharing. Otter.ai can assist with longer recordings.

Section 6 — Summative Assessment

Put It Into Practice

This final task asks you to apply what you've reviewed — not on a hypothetical, but on something real from your own work. You won't be assessed on perfection; you'll be assessed on thoughtfulness and understanding.

Your Task

  1. Select one digital artifact you've created recently — an email draft, a document, a slide deck, a form, or any digital file you use in your role.
  2. Review it against the Big 5: headings, alt text, color contrast, link text, and captions (if applicable).
  3. Identify at least two accessibility issues — or, if it's already accessible, describe two practices you applied intentionally.
  4. In the reflection box below, describe: (a) what you found, (b) what you changed or would change, and (c) why those changes matter for your users.

Sample Response

For this reflection, I chose a staff newsletter I created last month in Google Docs and distributed as a PDF. It's a two-page document I send to all building staff summarizing upcoming events, policy reminders, and professional learning opportunities.

When I reviewed it against the Big 5, I found two clear issues. First, I had used bold text and a larger font size to create section titles — "Upcoming Events," "PD Reminders," and so on — but I had not applied any actual Heading styles from the Paragraph Styles menu. This means that anyone using a screen reader would hear the entire newsletter read as one long block of body text, with no way to jump between sections or understand the structure. The fix is straightforward: I need to apply Heading 2 to each section title using the built-in style rather than formatting it manually.

Second, I included two hyperlinks in the document. One read "Click here to register" and another read "More information." Neither of these makes sense without the surrounding sentence, which means a screen reader user navigating by links would have no idea where they lead. I revised both: "Click here to register" became "Register for the October 14 PD Day (Google Form)" and "More information" became "Read the updated district leave policy (PDF)."

These changes matter because at least two staff members in my building use assistive technology — one uses a screen reader due to low vision, and another uses keyboard-only navigation after a repetitive strain injury. When my newsletter isn't structured accessibly, I'm creating barriers for colleagues I work with every day, and I'm doing it without realizing it. Applying real heading styles and writing descriptive links costs me almost no extra time but makes the document genuinely usable for everyone.
